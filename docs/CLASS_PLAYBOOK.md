## The system in one page  
  
He is not a breakout trader. **He is running a laddered accumulation book** — buying a  
falling market in measured slices, at levels fixed in advance, with the size of each  
slice derived from how far the market has already fallen, and exiting when it recovers.  
  
> *"If the market goes down, down, down — that is very, very good, because it comes back  
> quickly and you come out with a lot of profit."*  
  
The three things that make it a system rather than averaging down:  
  
1. **The levels are drawn before the money moves** — trendline, then Fibonacci, then  
 numbered boundaries.  
2. **Each boundary has its own fixed rupee budget**, computed from a formula, not from  
 conviction.  
3. **Each timeframe holds its own reserve** and may not spend another's.  
  
His own summary of what he is imitating:  
  
> *"Money keeps accumulating. Whatever the rich man does, that is what we are doing too."*

## Part 1 — Building the chart  
  
### 1.1 The step-by-step rule  
  
He opens the core lesson by attacking the common mistake:  
  
> *"You sit down and draw four or five trendlines all at once and then sit there  
> scratching your head. Stop. Go step by step."*  
  
The cycle, in order, and **nothing is drawn ahead of its turn**:  
  
1. Draw **one** trendline, high to high, coming down.  
2. Wait for price to **break out above** it.  
3. Wait for price to come back and **break the low**.  
4. **Only now** draw the Fibonacci for that leg, and the next trendline.  
5. Repeat.  
  
> *"Let the low break. Don't rush."*  
  
The low breaking is the confirmation that the move up is finished — *"only when the low  
breaks do we know it went up and came back down."*  
  
![Trendline and numbered levels](playbook-frames/01-trendline-and-levels.jpg)  
  
*His chart: BTCUSD 1-minute, a descending trendline off the swing high, horizontal  
levels labelled with an index and a price.*  
  
### 1.2 When a second trendline is needed  
  
Asked whether a new leg that doesn't touch the old line needs its own trendline:  
  
**Yes, always** — if the new low is **lower than the previous low**, even slightly, even  
on a 1-minute chart, it gets its own line.  
  
### 1.3 The shape of a usable trendline  
  
This is a filter, and one of the more useful things he says:  
  
| Shape | Verdict |  
|---|---|  
| **Escalator** — a steady staircase slope | What you want |  
| **Vertical / near-vertical** | Avoid |  
| **Too wide / spread out** | Avoid |  
  
> *"The market should be in an escalator model. If your trendline is straight up like  
> this, or spread out like that, avoid it."*  
  
His reason: a steep or wide line may take many days to be touched again, so you will  
get some trades but **miss far more**.  
  
### 1.4 Approaching a big chart quickly  
  
For a chart that has already travelled a long way, he gives a shortcut so you are not  
drawing from the beginning:  
  
1. Draw **high to low** first.  
2. Find where that line **collides** with price. Take the low **immediately before**  
 that collision and draw a line to it.  
3. Draw one more to the **high before that**.  
  
Two lines, and you can start. *"You don't need to draw one by one from the basics. That  
clarity helps, but it isn't necessary."*

## Part 2 — Entries  
  
### 2.1 The progression: candle → pattern → trend  
  
This is the spine of his entry method and he returns to it repeatedly.  
  
| Stage | What triggers the buy | When it applies |  
|---|---|---|  
| **Candle** | A candle closes beyond the level | The first, shallowest entries |  
| **Pattern** | A candle closes beyond the **pattern's** low | Deeper, once price has travelled |  
| **Trend** | The trendline itself | Deepest, and for large size |  
  
> *"Earlier you took it by candle. Now start taking it by pattern."*  
  
And a hard constraint on size:  
  
> *"From now on, when we do it big, we will not do candle or pattern. Only trend.  
> Then there is no mistake."*  
  
**So: small size may enter on a candle. Large size must wait for the trend.**  
  
### 2.2 The entry trigger itself  
  
Consistent across every lesson: **entry is on a candle CLOSE beyond the level, never on  
a touch.**  
  
> *"When this candle crosses and comes here — when this candle closes here — you can  
> place the order at this place."*  
  
For a buy back into a fallen market he adds a colour condition: the candle must  
**close green with its body inside the body/wick zone**, and cross beyond.  
  
### 2.3 The "settle" signal — three aggressive red candles  
  
Before buying a falling market he wants proof the fall is exhausting. This is the most  
precisely stated rule in the whole series:  
  
1. The **pattern or support must be broken** — candles that stay *inside* the pattern  
 do not count. *"One, two, three, four inside the pattern — that won't work."*  
2. **Minimum three consecutive big red candles** after the break.  
3. **At least one of those three must be larger than the biggest red candle so far**,  
 measured back to the mother candle.  
  
> *"Break the support and put continuously big red — bigger than whatever was biggest  
> before. Only then does it mean the market is going to settle. Then buy at this place."*  
  
He calls the aggressive fall *"control of fear"*, and says it typically happens **twice**  
before the market accumulates and turns.  
  
### 2.4 The biggest green candle  
  
Looking back from the high to the low, find the **largest green candle**. That is where  
buyers began pushing aggressively, and the location matters for where support sits.  
  
### 2.5 Don't chase the turn  
  
> *"When it goes down and turns back, don't buy in a rush."*  
  
Wait for the break and the re-emergence — pattern, then candle, then trend.

## Part 3 — Fund management: the arithmetic  
  
### 3.1 The capital formula  
  
This is the foundation, and it is simple:  
  
```  
rupees per 1% of market distance = capital ÷ 100  
```  
  
| Capital | Per 1% |  
|---|---|  
| ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,000 |  
| ₹50,000 | ₹500 |  
| ₹10,000 | ₹100 |  
  
> *"Capital divided by 100. That formula exists to find how much you should spend for  
> one percentage. That's all. After that, the calculation is over — then it's how much  
> real distance the market has."*  
  
### 3.2 The spreadsheet  
  
![The fund sheet](playbook-frames/03-the-fund-sheet.jpg)  
  
His sheet takes **HIGH and LOW only**; everything else fills automatically:  
  
| Column | Meaning |  
|---|---|  
| `RANGE` | HIGH − LOW |  
| `PERCENT` | RANGE ÷ HIGH, as a percent |  
| `BASE` | capital ÷ 100 (1000 on his sheet, i.e. a ₹1 lakh account) |  
| `RS` | PERCENT × BASE — **the rupees to deploy for that distance** |  
| `CV` | the increment over the previous row's RS |  
| `20 / 30 / 50` | slices of that increment |  
  
Worked example from his own sheet, which I checked:  
  
- High 65,843, low 65,661 → range 182 → 0.276% → **RS 276**  
- Next low 65,449 → 394 → 0.598% → RS 598, of which **270 is the new increment**  
- That 270 splits **20% = 54, 30% = 81, 50% = 135**  
  
**So 20/30/50 are portions of money to deploy, not profit targets.** This is the single  
thing I had wrong when reading the charts alone.  
  
### 3.3 Fund on boundaries  
  
![Fund on boundaries](playbook-frames/05-fund-on-boundaries.jpg)  
  
The numbered levels — **0, 1, 2, 4, 8** — each carry their own rupee budget from the  
sheet. The rules attached to them:  
  
- **Each boundary's money belongs to that boundary.** *"The money here can only be  
 placed here."*  
- **Keep them separate by default.** Combine two boundaries' funds **only if one alone  
 is not enough.**  
- **Split every deployment.** *"Don't put the whole amount in one place. Split it and  
 split it — your average price comes closer."*  
- **Buy only when a candle comes below the level**, not as it turns back up.  
- **Distance changes the size.** If levels 1 and 4 sit close together the average  
 repairs quickly. If 1 and 8 are far apart, the far one needs roughly **double**.

## Part 4 — Time Investment (his "7 to 10x")  
  
He calls this the most important video in the series, and the claim is specific:  
  
| Approach | Profit multiple *(his figures, unverified)* |  
|---|---|  
| Random entries | 2–3x |  
| Money management only | 3–5x |  
| **Money + time investment** | **7–10x** |  
  
### The technique  
  
**Reset the chart so that your starting point becomes the first candle** — the mother  
candle. Then the visible low on each timeframe is unambiguous.  
  
Then, for each timeframe in turn:  
  
1. Set the mother candle as the first candle.  
2. Find the **visible low** on that timeframe.  
3. **Deploy that timeframe's money only below that low.**  
  
> *"Only when it comes below this do you try buying. Not before. That is the real time  
> investment."*  
  
Run 1m → 5m → 15m → 1H, each with its own low and its own reserve.  
  
### The reserve rule  
  
This is the part that stops it becoming ordinary averaging, and it is stated hard:  
  
> *"If the 15-minute order is still active, you must not use that money."*  
  
The 1-hour fund may only be spent **below where the 1-hour boundary begins**. And the  
warning that follows:  
  
> *"If you use all the money on 1-minute, you will not have fund management at any  
> large scale."*  
  
He suggests keeping **two demo accounts** to practise connecting the timeframes.

## Part 5 — The 30/70 rule and the reserve  
  
### 5.1 The three-buy ladder  
  
Within a zone he buys three times, in proportion:  
  
| Where | Share |  
|---|---|  
| First touch | **20%** |  
| On the return | **30%** |  
| At the deeper level | **50%** |  
  
That ladder consumes **50% of capital**. The 30/70 lesson is about **cutting it to  
30%** and getting the same result:  
  
> *"What I was getting using 50,000, I should now get using 20–30,000."*  
  
### 5.2 The drawdown table  
  
Worked through with the class, for a ₹1 lakh account:  
  
| Market falls | Deployed | Note |  
|---|---|---|  
| 25% | ₹12,500 | the other ₹12,500 waits for 50% |  
| 50% | ₹25,000 | |  
| 100% | ₹50,000 | **the maximum that is ever spent on trading** |  
  
**The remaining 50% is never a trading fund.** It is reserved for the *investment zone*  
below the 50% line. His reason is about income, not about the trade:  
  
> *"If the market is drastically down 50–60%, for nearly two or three months there will  
> be no income at any size. That backup is what brings income in that period."*  
  
### 5.3 Save versus rescue  
  
A distinction he draws explicitly and which is worth keeping:  
  
- **Reducing risk** — sizing so the position never becomes dangerous.  
- **Rescuing** — repairing a position that already is.  
  
> *"Making risk small is one thing. Rescuing risk is another thing. They are different  
> in nature."*  
  
### 5.4 Exit on the book, not the leg  
  
> *"Don't cut individually. I sell only when the overall profit comes."*  
  
A 25% zone gives roughly 10% of distance *(heard once)*; four such moves compound into  
the result he is describing. Individual legs are not managed — **the book is**.  
  
![Profit zones](playbook-frames/04-profit-zones.jpg)  
  
*"1st Profit Zone" and "2nd Profit Zone" marked on his 5-minute chart, with the slice  
numbers beside them.*

## Part 6 — What is opinion, not rule  
  
Kept separate deliberately, because it is stated with the same force as the rules:  
  
- Three months of serious practice and the business "serves you for life".  
- 100 demo trades, then 100 live trades, before he considers you competent.  
- Notes are mandatory — *"the only holy thing that wins here is notes"*.  
- You cannot beat professionals with margin, and margin will not accelerate you.  
- Intraday is a **wholesale market**, there for liquidity, not for daily income  
 *(from the August Day16_2 lesson)*.  
- Loss rate by holding period: short-term **7 in 10 negative**, medium ~50%, long ~20%  
 *(his live class poll, not data)*.

## Day36 — the micro boundary, and the first exit that is not a target  
  
*Source: `Day36_Prof_Fund_Mgmt_Multiple_Entries`, 84 min, transcribed 25-Sep-2026.  
Clean: 6 bad characters, top token 2.82%, 2.6% degraded — the best of the set.*  
  
**The "micro boundary" is his name for the ladder's working range.** It had not  
appeared under that name before:  
  
> *"Split that trade, place it as multiple trades, for the same amount of  
> profit… within this short-term micro boundary, my aim is to come out with  
> overall profit."*  
  
So a single intended position is deliberately broken into several, all of them  
living inside one bounded stretch of price, and the book leaves on the **total**.  
That is T7 again, but stated as a design decision rather than an exit rule: he is  
not averaging down a losing trade, he is *planning* the entry as several.  
  
**And the first genuine risk control in the whole course:**  
  
> *"If you need an emergency exit, you can… cut immediately, partial close."*  
  
Fifteen lessons produced no stop loss at all, and Day30 gave an optional  
intraday one. This adds a third option — **closing part of the book** — which is  
neither a stop nor a target. It is the only mechanism he offers for reducing  
exposure without ending the campaign. He does not attach a trigger to it, so it  
stays an instruction rather than a rule.  
  
The averaging arithmetic appears again (*"even if the market goes further down,  
your average price…"*), consistent with T10 and T17, and the 30/70 split is  
discussed as a **single entry** rather than a ladder — the same distinction  
Day33 draws.

## Day26 — a trailing rule, gated at half the move  
  
*Source: `Day26_Trend_Entry_Techniques`, 41 min, transcribed 24-Sep-2026. Clean:  
1 bad character, top token 2.45%, 4.6% degraded.*  
  
Two things, one of them new.  
  
**New — the trail has a gate:**  
  
> *"Only after 50% has come do you take the trail."*  
  
Nothing before this lesson mentioned trailing at all. It is not a stop that  
follows from entry: the position is left alone until the move is **half way to  
its objective**, and only then does anything start following it. That fits the  
rest of the system, which never protects an open position early — the ladder has  
no price stop and the intraday stop is optional.  
  
**Confirmed, independently — the average-entry exit.** Day42 Part 2 is no longer  
the only source for T17:  
  
> *"This bought, and the 50 target was reached. Even averaging these two, 0.25  
> was reached."*  
  
Same arithmetic, different lesson, different day: legs are averaged, and the  
book's target is read off that average. He also refers to building *"a 1, 2, 3  
trading system"* around the 0.5 — consistent with the doubling rungs, though the  
passage is too thin to pin as a rule.

## Day24 — what "trend" actually means, and why big size waits for it  
  
*Source: `Day24_Trend_Concept`, 71 min, transcribed 24-Sep-2026. Clean: 3 bad  
characters, top token 2.01%, 3.0% degraded. Ten mentions of trend across two  
spellings — searching one spelling finds two and reads as an empty lesson.*  
  
The trendline set says large size may only enter on the **trend**, never on a  
candle or a pattern, but never defines the word. Day24 does, and it is not a  
geometric definition — it is a definition by **patience and clarity**:  
  
> *"I have waited up to this point. **This** is the trend."*  
> *"Is everything clear? Then this is the trend."*  
  
A trend is the level that survives long enough to stop being ambiguous. That is  
consistent with Day42's *"breaking a trendline is not a simple thing — it takes  
that much time"*: both lessons treat elapsed time as the evidence.  
  
**The observation that earns its place** is about where the lines land:  
  
> *"When you draw the trend… the pattern also comes at the same place. Almost  
> all of them come close together."*  
  
So the pattern line and the trend line **converge on nearly the same level**. That  
reframes the progression: candle → pattern → trend are not three different prices,  
they are the same neighbourhood confirmed three times over, each with more time  
behind it. Which is exactly why size is allowed to grow along it — you are not  
reaching further, you are paying for more confirmation at the same place.  
  
He also sketches the trade-frequency cost of going up a timeframe — *"here you get  
ten trades, here only five, here three or two"* — against a larger profit per  
trade. The specific multiples are too garbled in this transcript to quote, and the  
one 4-hour passage does not survive transcription cleanly.

## Day42 Part 2 — THE EXIT ARITHMETIC, in his own words  
  
*Source: `Day42_..._Part2`, transcribed 24-Sep-2026, 70 min. Quality acceptable but  
noisier than the others: 5.9% degraded lines, and this passage in particular loops  
("has it gone 0.25? has it gone 0.25?"). The substance below is stated across several  
independent lines, which is why I trust it despite the noise.*  
  
**This is the lesson that proves the exit rule Phil gave on 24-Sep.** He is trading  
live and narrating each exit:  
  
> *"I bought at this place — at its **0.25** my target has been reached."*  
> *"**This is my mother candle.**"*  
  
The 0.25 is stated immediately beside the mother candle, which is exactly the  
measurement Phil described: a fraction of the distance from entry back toward the  
mother.  
  
> *"What did I do? Bought at **2** — it sold for me. Next, bought at **4** — sold at  
> the **0.5** target."*  
  
Two things fall out of that sentence:  
  
1. **The boundary indices ARE the buy levels.** The `0 / 1 / 2 / 4 / 8` labels on his  
 charts — the doubling series I decoded from the frames but could not explain — are  
 the rungs of the ladder. He buys *at* them and names them when he exits.  
2. **Both targets are live at once**, per leg. He checks 0.25 first ("at 4, has it  
 gone 0.25?") and takes 0.5 when the deeper leg runs further.  
  
And then the piece that ties the whole system together:  
  
> *"When everything adds up together… its **average price plus its 0.25**."*  
  
**That is T10 confirmed from the source.** Individual legs aim at their own 0.25; once  
the ladder has several rungs, the book is worked off the **average price** — which is  
precisely why deeper ladders exit at lower prices, and why he calls a falling market  
good.

## The exit rule — and why there is no stop  
  
*Source: Phil, 24-Sep-2026. This is the one part of the system that did not come out of  
the transcripts, and it is the part that makes the rest testable.*  
  
This is **investment-style trading with no stop loss**, deliberately. It is the same  
premise the CryptoForge engines were built on, so the design is already familiar: a  
ladder is not defended with a price stop, it is defended with unspent capital.  
  
**Exits are taken at 0.25 and 0.5 of the distance from the average entry back toward  
the mother candle.** So the target is measured off the *book*, not off any single leg,  
which is why T7 (exit on total book profit) and this rule are the same rule seen from  
two sides — as the ladder adds legs, the average entry moves down, and both targets move  
down with it.  
  
Worked example — mother candle high at 100, average entry after three buys at 80:  
  
| | Calculation | Exit price |  
|---|---|---|  
| Distance to recover | 100 − 80 = 20 | |  
| First exit, 0.25 | 80 + (20 × 0.25) | **85** |  
| Second exit, 0.5 | 80 + (20 × 0.5) | **90** |  
  
Two consequences worth stating before any backtest:  
  
- **Deeper ladders exit sooner in price terms.** Every new leg pulls the average entry  
 down, which pulls both targets down. A book that has laddered a long way needs a much  
 smaller bounce to hit 0.25 than a shallow one does — which is exactly why he says a  
 falling market is *good*.  
- **The split between the two exits is not yet fixed.** How much of the book leaves at  
 0.25 versus 0.5 is a free parameter, and Phil's position is that the backtest decides  
 it rather than the lessons. Treat the ratio as something to sweep, not something known.

## Day15 — the V pattern, and why an order is NOT placed on the candle  
  
Day15 is the first lesson where the entry is taken **off a pattern instead of  
off a candle**, and he is explicit that this is an upgrade, not an alternative.  
His own framing of the course is a ladder: *"first candle, then pattern, then  
trend"* — and he says the pattern version means **fewer trades and more  
profit**, which is the same trade-off Day24 makes for trend.  
  
**The four V's.** He names them and only the first is tradable:  
  
| | |  
|---|---|  
| **V pattern** | down leg, a red close, then a **completed green 5-minute close** — two legs and a head |  
| **Equal V** | the second leg cannot get past the first; price stalls at 25/50/75 of the way |  
| **Failed V** | the base breaks instead of holding |  
| **Extra V** | the move carries beyond the V's own head |  
  
Only the plain V is treated as a signal. The other three are named so they can  
be *recognised and left alone* — the same purpose the "skip" cases serve in  
the trendline set.  
  
**The candle method, as a contrast.** He replays the old way first: the moment  
the red candle closes, the order goes in at that price, and the target is 0.25  
measured from the ultimate high. He then says plainly **not to do this any  
more** — *"don't rush the order in like before, that is the candle method."*  
  
**The pattern method — the rule.** Three steps, in order:  
  
1. Wait for the **completed green 5-minute close** that finishes the V. Not a  
 green candle in progress. *"Only when the five minutes are finished and it  
 closes."*  
2. **Mark the extreme head — the low of the V — and leave the mark alone.**  
 The same discipline as the mother candle, which he restates here: mark it  
 clearly once, *"never change it."*  
3. Require a candle to **close outside that mark with at least 50% of its body  
 beyond it**. A candle that pokes outside without a 50% body close is *not*  
 the break — *"wait, no problem"* — and the order then goes on the **previous  
 candle's** level.  
  
That 50% body condition is stated three separate times in this lesson and is  
the strictest version of T1 anywhere in the course so far.  
  
**The timeframe escalation — the same pattern, three times.** After the 5m  
entry he repeats the identical test on higher timeframes, each one adding size:  
  
- **15m** — a green 15m close, then the second green close, then the 50%  
 outside close. *If the 5-minute order already sits at that price, no extra  
 order is placed* — the level, not the timeframe, is what is being bought.  
- **1H** — a green 1H close; order placed above it once the hour completes.  
  
**The exit is measured from the LAST buy, not the average.** This is the one  
place Day15 contradicts what Phil confirmed for the ladder (T10/T17). His words  
here: *"don't average — where did you buy last? Take the whole order out at  
0.25, and 0.5 is right there."* On the escalated adds the reference is the last  
fill back toward the mother candle.  
  
**Alerts instead of staring.** Set an alert at the green candle's level; when it  
fires, go and check for the 50% outside close. He is dismissive of the  
alternative — *"this looking every 5 minutes, every 10 minutes, that is not  
work at all."*  
  
**Why no stop, stated again and differently.** Day30 gave the intraday answer;  
Day15 gives the market-structure one. Stops get hunted — on BTC, gold and forex  
especially, and on news *"it will be terrifying"* — price takes the stop out and  
returns, *"the stop need not have been hit at all."* Recovery is by adding, not  
by a stop. Alongside it, the sizing pep-talk: start with one lakh, earn, build  
to two, use the 50x margin the broker gives — and the line he clearly likes:  
**money is a butterfly; chase it and you never catch it, sit relaxed and it  
comes.**

## Day16 (parts 1–4) — the arithmetic behind candle / pattern / trend  
  
Day15 said the pattern method means "fewer trades, more profit". Day16 puts  
numbers on that claim, and they are the most quantitative thing in the course  
so far. This is the lesson that explains WHY the ladder exists.  
  
**The trade-off, in his own figures.** Take the same account and the same  
market, and the three levels differ like this:  
  
| | trades available | money committed | time committed |  
|---|---|---|---|  
| **Candle** | 100 | 70% | 30% |  
| **Pattern** | 50 | 50% | 50% |  
| **Trend** | 20 | 30% | 70% |  
  
The point he drives at: *the same money comes out of all three.* Trading the  
candle gives five times the trades of the trend for the same return — you just  
pay for it with capital instead of patience. His summary line is blunt: the  
belief that you must **put in more money to take out more money is wrong** —  
what changes the outcome is the LEVEL you trade at, not the size you deploy.  
This is the arithmetic underneath T3's 20/30/50 split and the 30/70 rule.  
  
**Why a pattern works at all — the 30% rule.** He gives a probability, not a  
certainty: for a red candle to turn, *"30% buyer involvement is enough"* — the  
sellers do not have to be beaten, only interrupted. At **50%** the odds tip the  
other way: *"if it is 50%, there is a chance this continues"* — i.e. half the  
force behind it means the move extends rather than reverses. That is the first  
time anywhere in the course that a number is attached to the read.  
  
**The measured move.** Once a V completes, the travel is counted in the same  
ladder every time: **25, 50, 75, 100** and on through **125, 150, 175, 200**.  
He does not treat these as targets so much as stations — the question at each  
one is whether the force is still there.  
  
**And a trend is just patterns, repeated.** *"More than one of these patterns in  
a row is what a trend is."* So the three levels are not three systems; they are  
one read at three magnifications — which is why the entry rules do not change  
between them, only the size and the waiting.  
  
**Live demo notes (parts 3 and 4).** He trades gold live during the session and  
the mechanics repeat Day15's exactly: the order goes **only after a green candle  
closes with 50% of its body outside** — *"your rule needs green; only on green  
can an entry be placed, otherwise reject it"* — the stop-order price is typed  
from the marked low, and *"the high is always what counts"* when marking the  
structure, never the body. He also repeats the discipline that matters most on  
a live chart: **do not go hunting.** *"You should not be sitting and searching  
everywhere"* — the setup is either at the level you already marked, or it is  
not yours.

|---|---|  
| **T25** | A red candle needs only **~30% opposing force to turn**; at ~50% the move **continues** instead | Partly — "force" needs a proxy (volume? body ratio?) before this can be measured |  
| **T26** | The post-pattern move travels in **25/50/75/100…** steps of the pattern's own height | Yes — a measured-move target ladder |

## Day20 part 2 — where the money LIVES when it is not in a trade  
  
This one is treasury, not trading, and it is the most practical file in the  
course so far. It answers a question the rest of the system leaves open: the  
capital you are deliberately not risking has to sit somewhere.  
  
**Not with the broker.** *"Do not keep your surplus with Zerodha. If you keep it  
there it sits in Zerodha's own bundle, and if the broker goes under…"* Keep only  
the working margin at the broker; the reserve leaves the platform.  
  
**Laddered fixed deposits, not one big one.** His worked example: of five lakh,  
put **three lakh in a three-year FD and two lakh in a one-year FD** — and more

single deposit.  
  
**The reason is the emergency, not the interest.** *"If you need one or two  
lakh, break only that FD. If you need three, break only that one. The others go  
on growing without the interest penalty."* One deposit broken early costs you  
the rate on the whole sum; several let you break the smallest piece that covers  
the need.  
  
**Auto-renewal, interest reinvested.** Set the deposits to renew at maturity  
with the interest rolled back in, so the reserve compounds without attention.  
  
**The market equivalent is a liquid fund** — he names liquid/mutual-fund  
products as the same idea for money that must stay reachable.  
  
One line I could not decode cleanly: something to the effect that the target is  
your salary or business income over two months. The transcript is too broken  
there to state as a rule, so it is left out rather than guessed at.  
  
Nothing testable — but this is the part of the system that decides whether a  
drawdown is survivable, and it belongs beside T6's reserve rule.  
  
## Day20 part 3 — correlation as a hedge, and the only way he says you lose  
  
Short Q\&A file. Two things in it are real; the rest is encouragement.  
  
**Hold two correlated instruments that move against each other.** *"On one side  
cash-related, on the other side gold — start doing the same pattern on both.  
When this goes up that comes down; when this goes down that comes up. That is  
the whole thing, and it applies to every tool in the world."* His weather  
analogy: a fierce sun brings the rain. So the book is not one instrument read  
well, it is a **pair chosen so that one of them is always working**, each traded  
with the same pattern method.  
  
He sketches a third leg too — the market itself — but the transcript is too  
broken there to state what it was.  
  
**The only way you lose, in his telling, is greed.** *"Greed — loss comes from  
greed. There is no other place you can lose from."* Blunt, and consistent with  
the no-stop design: if the size is right and the reserve is real, the thing that  
kills the account is wanting more, not being wrong.  
  
**And the timeline he actually recommends: six months of practice before  
trading, and he says of himself that for the FIRST FIFTEEN YEARS he only did  
investment.** That is worth sitting next to every backtest — the man teaching  
this did not day-trade for fifteen years.  
  
### What it adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T32** | Trade a **correlated pair** (his example: a cash-side instrument and gold) with the same method, so one side is working when the other is not | Yes in principle — but it needs a second instrument's data, and everything we hold is NIFTY |  
  
T32 is the first rule in the course that our data cannot test at all. Worth  
noting rather than quietly skipping: the pair is central to how he runs money,  
and we have only ever measured one leg of it.  
  
## Day21 — the add-on rules: two V's, the fib over the whole depth, and the cap  
  
This is the lesson that tells the ladder when it is allowed to add, and it  
answers two things our backtest had to guess at.  
  
**Wait for TWO V patterns below before buying.** *"Below this, two V patterns  
must come. One has come, the second has not — then wait."* If the second never  
forms, *"we let the 5-minute go"* — the setup is abandoned rather than forced.  
Our ladder adds at a price level; his adds at a level **that has been confirmed  
twice by structure**. That alone would cut the number of legs hard.  
  
**Draw a fibonacci over the WHOLE depth, then check the 0.25.** *"Where the  
mother candle is, where the low is — the whole range. First draw a fibonacci  
over the entire depth. Then see whether your pattern is above 0.25 or not."* So  
the 0.25 is not only an exit fraction (T10); it is also an **admission test for  
a new leg** — a pattern forming below the 0.25 of the whole move is not the same  
animal as one forming above it.  
  
**Money per timeframe, and it is small.** He gives working numbers: about  
**2,000 on the 5-minute, 3,000 on the 1-hour**, and within a 2,000 allocation  
the legs split **1,000 / 1,000**. Two legs per timeframe, not five.  
  
**Never add above the boundary.** *"Beyond this boundary, do not take an entry  
upward."* The ladder only ever buys lower, which is what stops it becoming a  
breakout chase.  
  
**When the target is missed and price comes back inside the boundary, drop to  
the candle method** — trade the small structure between the zero band and the  
0.5 line instead of waiting for the big target.  
  
**And the exit is joint.** *"Sell both buys entirely."* Consistent with T7: the  
book leaves together, legs are not managed separately.  
  
### What Day21 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T33** | A leg may only be added after **two V patterns** have formed below the last one | Yes — needs the V detector, which Day15 defines |  
| **T34** | Draw the fib over the **whole depth (mother to low)**; a leg is only valid **above the 0.25** of it | Yes |  
| **T35** | Two legs per timeframe, split 1,000/1,000 of a ~2,000 allocation — not the five-boundary ladder we modelled | Yes |  
  
**T33 and T35 both say our ladder over-trades.** We modelled five boundary legs  
filling on price alone; he waits for two V's per add and stops at two legs. The  
backtest's median campaign was one leg and its deep boundaries never filled —  
which now looks less like a flaw in the model and more like the model being  
pointed at the wrong thing entirely.  
  
## Day22 — the mother candle RESETS, and the timeframes hand over  
  
A question-and-answer file, but the question is the one our model gets wrong.  
  
**A new mother candle replaces the old one.** A student asks what to do when  
another mother candle forms beyond the current one, and the answer is flat:  
*"then that is the mother candle. That's all."* The reference is not fixed for  
the life of a campaign — it moves as the structure moves, and every distance  
measured from it (the 0.25 and 0.5 exits, the fib over the whole depth) moves  
with it. He adds the consequence: you cannot take an entry above the new mother;  
you wait for a pattern or a candle to form below it.  
  
**When a level gives way, the next timeframe takes over.** *"If it goes past  
this, the 15-minute chart's pattern will also break and go below — so keep  
following the pattern."* The ladder does not defend a broken level; it steps up  
a timeframe and works the larger structure. That is what makes the 1m → 5m →  
15m → 1H progression a single system rather than four separate books.  
  
### What Day22 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T36** | The mother candle is **re-marked whenever a new one forms beyond it**, and all targets move with it | Yes — and our backtest does NOT do this |  
  
**T36 is a real gap in `tools/ladder_backtest.py`.** It fixes the mother at the  
moment a campaign opens and never moves it, so on a long campaign it measures  
the 0.25 exit back to a structure the market left behind weeks ago. On the 2% of  
campaigns that run past a month — the ones holding 59% of all the notional-days,  
and therefore paying most of the carry — that is precisely the wrong reference.  
Re-marking would pull those targets down and close those campaigns far sooner.  
  
## Day23 — "1, 2, 3", and the first real student numbers  
  
A results session. Two things in it are worth keeping.  
  
**What "1, 2, 3" means.** A student says it back and is corrected into shape:  
**one entry on the 5-minute, two on the 15-minute**, and *"3 means if you get one  
pattern and two candles, you can take it."* So the numbers are **entries  
permitted per timeframe**, not a sequence — which is the same cap Day21 puts at  
two legs per timeframe, seen from the other side.  
  
**Three students, one market, three results: ₹15,000, ₹32,000 and ₹35,000.**  
His point is the spread, not the size — *"that is the beauty of the market:  
where is 15,000, where is 32,000, where is 35,000?"* Same instrument, same week,  
same rules, three outcomes. Worth holding on to when any backtest reports a  
single number as though the method determines the result.  
  
**And the holding periods are real.** The gold trades being closed that day had  
been open **a week to ten days**, in profit. That is the correlated-pair leg  
(T32) running over days, not intraday — which matches what our own ladder found:  
the campaigns that matter are not the ones that close in an hour.  
  
No new mechanics. The 1/2/3 cap restates T35.  
  
## Day23 part 2 — "carve it in stone": 1-1, 1-2, 1-4, 1-4-8  
  
The clearest sizing statement in the whole course, and he tells them to memorise  
it like an inscription:  
  
> *"1-1 trading system, 1-2 trading system, 1-4 trading system, 1-4-8. That is  
> it."*  
  
**And the one that loses: 1-1-1.** *"1-1-1 is the system that fails."* Equal-sized  
adds are the losing version — the legs must GROW. Which means the 0/1/2/4/8 of  
T18 has been doing double duty all along: it is the spacing of the levels **and  
the multiple of the size at each one**. Our ladder sized its legs 20/30/50 of a  
zone's fund; his multiplies 1, then 4, then 8.  
  
**Why a "losing system" can still win, in his telling:** *"I have often said we  
can win with a losing system… because there you do not only do investment, you  
add TIME investment."* The sizing ladder alone is a martingale and he knows it;  
what he claims rescues it is the timeframe discipline — deploying each  
timeframe's fund only below that timeframe's low, so the adds are spread across  
structures rather than fired off in one collapse.  
  
The class anecdote attached to it: a student from Dharmapuri who started with  
₹20,000 and has just bought a BMW, with the business paying him about three lakh  
a month. *(His figures, unverified, and the sort of claim that should stay in  
the "opinion" column no matter how often it is repeated.)*  
  
### What Day23 part 2 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T37** | Leg sizes MULTIPLY — 1-1, 1-2, 1-4 or 1-4-8 — and **1-1-1 (equal adds) is the losing version** | Yes, and it is a sweep: four named ladders against each other |  
  
**T37 changes the ladder backtest materially.** Everything measured last night  
used 20/30/50 sizing, which is closest to 1-1.5-2.5 — flatter than any system he  
names, and much flatter than 1-4-8. A martingale ladder with no stop is also  
exactly where the drawdown risk lives, so this is the parameter that decides  
whether the thing is survivable, not just profitable. It needs running against  
the same six years before anyone treats 1-4-8 as safe.  
  
## Day23 part 3 — the low is dragged, the mother is not  
  
Short file that settles something the earlier days left ambiguous, and it  
matters for how the ladder is coded.  
  
**The LOW moves with the market.** *"As the next low comes, keep changing the  
low. Wherever the visible low appears, keep dragging it along with you."* The  
buying reference is not fixed at the start of a campaign — it follows each new  
low down, which is what makes "deploy only below the visible low" (T4) a moving  
condition rather than a one-off test.  
  
**The MOTHER does not move when you change timeframe.** *"The same mother candle  
does not change — the same one."* Dropping to the 15-minute for a clearer view  
is a change of magnification, not a change of reference.  
  
Put beside Day22 — where a new mother candle forming BEYOND the old one does  
replace it — the rule resolves cleanly:  
  
> **The mother is re-marked only when the structure itself is exceeded. Looking  
> at it on another timeframe never re-marks it. The visible low, by contrast,  
> is dragged continuously.**  
  
So the ladder has **two references moving at different speeds**: a sticky one  
the exits are measured from, and a running one the entries are measured from.  
Our backtest froze both.  
  
**0.25 is the unit of account throughout.** *"We will only buy if it goes 0.25…  
here at the close, 0.25 is our reckoning… place the order at 0.25, and 0.5."*  
The same fraction admits a leg (T34), sets the first exit (T10) and measures the  
travel (T26) — it is one number doing three jobs, which is why the system feels  
smaller than it looks written down.  
  
**And the patience rule beneath it:** *"Below 50%, do it. No hurry."* Fear and  
greed live above the halfway line; the work happens below it.  
  
No new testable rule — but T36 is now precise, and `tools/ladder_backtest.py`  
contradicts it twice over.  
  
## Day25 — "there is no such thing as 15-minute or 5-minute in this"  
  
The trend series opens by demolishing the idea that the timeframes are separate  
systems:  
  
> *"There is no such thing as 15-minute or 5-minute in this. I will even go to  
> one hour. Even at one hour, THE MOTHER CANDLE IS MY RECKONING."*  
  
The sequence he describes: you are working the 5-minute; the move goes further  
than the 5-minute can show; you step to the 15-minute — *"even at 15 minutes,  
that low is still there"* — and if needed to the hour. The mother candle does  
not change at any point. *"We will join back to this low, and the trade starts  
from there automatically."* And a close is a close at every magnification:  
*"when I put a close here, it is put on the 5-minute, on the 1-hour, on the  
1-day."*  
  
His practical advice for reading it: drop back to the 5-minute to read the  
individual candles, because the structure is easier to see there — the  
magnification is for your eyes, not for the trade.  
  
### What this means for our model, and it is not small  
  
`tools/ladder_backtest.py` ran **four independent books** — 1m, 5m, 15m and 60m  
— each with its own mother, its own campaigns and its own capital, and then  
added the results up. Day25 says that is the wrong shape. There is **ONE  
campaign on ONE mother candle**; the timeframe decides only **which fund is  
released** as price travels further from it (Part 4's reserve rule), not which  
trade you are in.  
  
So the four numbers reported last night are four parallel simulations of a  
system that is meant to be one. Whether that flatters or hurts the result is  
genuinely unclear — one campaign drawing on four funds in sequence is a deeper  
ladder than any single book we ran, which cuts both ways: more staying power,  
and more money committed to a structure that has already been wrong three times.  
  
No new rule number: this sharpens T4 and T36 rather than adding to them.  
  
## Day25 part 2 — "under value": the 50% line on every timeframe at once  
  
The homework he sets, and it is the most mechanical thing in the course:  
  
1. Take the **1-minute** chart and **reset** it.  
2. Find the **visible highest high and lowest low**.  
3. **Drag a fibonacci from the LOW to the HIGH.**  
4. Use only **0, 50 and 100** — *"25 is not wanted, that is a minor boundary."*  
5. Repeat on the **5-minute, the 15-minute and the 1-hour**.  
6. Then ask one question: *"50% is here for the 1-minute, here for the  
 5-minute, here for the 15-minute — is it under value for all three? Below 50?  
 Below 50 means UNDER VALUE."*  
  
When every timeframe agrees that price sits below its own 50% line, the market  
is "under value" and that is where the buying happens — *"and with 0.25 on this,  
the buy has been taken."*  
  
This is a **filter we have never tested, and it is entirely testable.** It needs  
no pattern recognition, no V's, no candle reading: four fibonacci retracements  
and an AND. It is also the first rule in the course that explains why he keeps  
insisting the timeframes are one system — the condition is defined ACROSS them,  
so no single timeframe can produce it alone.  
  
### What Day25 part 2 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T38** | Buy only when price is **below the 50% of the visible low-to-high range on EVERY timeframe** (1m, 5m, 15m, 1H) — his "under value" | **Yes**, and cleanly — it is arithmetic on four ranges |  
  
**T38 is the next thing to backtest.** Every ladder result so far was gated on a  
mother candle and a boundary; none of them asked whether the market was under  
value on all four clocks at once. And unlike the confluence detector, this one  
does not depend on my reading of a pattern — it is his words turned directly  
into a comparison, which is exactly the kind of rule that survives or dies  
cleanly.  
  
## Day27 part 1 — only three lines, and what "extension 2" means  
  
Mostly fund management, but it fixes the drawing discipline that Day25's  
exercise leaves loose, and it gives a second definition of "under value".  
  
**Three lines, never more.** *"25-50 and all that is not wanted. Only three lines  
should be there — 0, 1, 2. That is all."* On the structure fib you keep the  
**0 line, the 100 line and the extension 2**; anything else *"and your chart  
becomes a mess."*  
  
**The pattern fib carries different lines: only 25 and 50.** *"On this one, only  
the 25 and the 50 should be there. Putting a 2 on this, putting a 4 on this —  
those lines must not go here."* So there are two overlays doing two jobs, and  
mixing their lines is the error he is guarding against:  
  
| Overlay | Lines | Job |  
|---|---|---|  
| **Structure** (low → high) | 0, 100, **extension 2** | where the move is going |  
| **Pattern** (the leg you are trading) | **25, 50** | where to buy and where to leave |  
  
**And the equivalence worth having:** *"automatically we come to under value  
below 50 — the same way, when this pattern's extension 2 arrives, that is under  
value for it. Both are one and the same."* So "below the 50 of the structure"  
and "the pattern has extended to 2" are two readings of the same condition — the  
first measured on the big range, the second on the leg. That matters for T38,  
because it means the under-value test can be computed either way and the two  
should agree.  
  
**The floor on the first buy:** *"at minimum you must go and buy at 25"*, and  
once that 25 is bought *"automatically this stops"* — the first leg is not taken  
before the 25, and taking it closes that stage.  
  
No new rule number — this sharpens T38 and T34 and tells us how to draw them  
without contradicting each other.  
  
## Day28 — the cost lesson, and his own answer to carry: SPOT  
  
The longest file in the set and the most badly degenerated by the transcriber  
(long runs of a single syllable), but the substance survives intact and it lands  
directly on the arithmetic our backtests have been arguing about. This is the  
lesson about **what a trade costs before it is allowed to be called profit.**  
  
### The threshold, in his own numbers  
  
He works it through on a gold order and the conclusion is a hard line:  
  
> *"If it falls one percent — in this trade you have neither profit nor loss."*  
  
One percent of travel **is** the cost. Two percent leaves you half a percent.  
Brokerage on a one-lakh round trip he puts at about ₹20 each way, and then  
*"there is STT, there is everything."* So:  
  
| Move captured | What is left |  
|---|---|  
| **1%** | nothing — costs exactly |  
| **2%** | about **0.5%** |  
  
And the framing he repeats: there are **two** bills, not one — *"one is the  
investment cost, the second is expenses; cover both, and only what comes after  
that is profit."*  
  
### The four ways to hold the same thing, and what each charges  
  
This is the clearest inventory in the course, and he gives it for gold and for  
Bitcoin identically — *"in gold there is a future, in gold there is an option;  
in Bitcoin there is a future, an option, a spot, a margin."*  
  
| | Leverage | Interest | Expiry | Hold for |  
|---|---|---|---|---|  
| **Spot** | none | **none** | **none** | as long as you like |  
| **Margin** | 50:1, 10:1 | **every 24 hours**, at a cut-off time | none | as long as you like, paying |  
| **Future** | yes | in the price | **yes** | to expiry, then pay |  
| **Option** | yes | brutal | **yes** | *"for a ten-rupee thing he makes you pay twelve; sell it and you get three"* |  
  
On margin he is precise about the mechanic: the broker lends the other 9,000 of a  
10,000 position, and *"every 24 hours you pay the charge"*, reset at the seller's  
own cut-off time. Then the judgement:  
  
> *"Only if you can bring in income exceeding all those costs should you take  
> the loan."*  
  
And on paying up for a full lot in ignorance: *"that is a fool buying without  
knowing — this is debt at usurious interest; bank interest is seven percent,  
this is worse."*  
  
### What this settles for us, and it is not small  
  
**We costed the ladder on FUTURES and the carry ate it.** Of a 13,083-point bill  
in the 5.6-year run, **7,951 was carry and rollover — futures-only tolls.** Day28  
says his own answer to that is the first row of the table: **spot.** No interest,  
no expiry, hold as long as the ladder needs. A system built on *"there is no stop  
loss, you recover by adding"* needs unlimited time, and only spot sells unlimited  
time for free.  
  
That is the same conclusion arrived at independently on 26-Sep-2026 when asking  
why NIFTY BEES was never tested — and it means BEES is not a substitute for what  
he teaches, it is **closer to it than futures are.**  
  
**The student question that opens the file** is our T35 question, asked by  
someone else: *"when it goes 2, 4, 8 like that — can we take one trade at 2 and  
one trade at 4?"* The answer is that he told them **two only**, and *"then why  
does the question of 4 and 8 arise?"* The boundary ladder is a map of levels, not  
a licence for a leg at each one. Third confirmation of the two-leg cap.  
  
**Practice, and the tool.** *"You must draw it five hundred times, then the flow  
comes."* A student says he cannot draw on his phone: *"then learn to draw one, or  
buy a laptop — do one of the two."* (The by-play about sitting next to his  
mother-in-law is worth nothing but it is genuinely funny.)  
  
### What Day28 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T39** | A trade that captures **1% has made nothing** — that is the cost line; 2% leaves about 0.5%. Any target below ~1% of price is not a trade | **Yes**, and it is a filter on the 0.25 exit: check what fraction of price the 0.25 target actually is |  
| **T40** | Choose the instrument by what it charges for TIME: **spot pays nothing and never expires**; margin charges every 24h; futures and options expire. Borrow only if the edge beats the interest | **Yes** — re-cost the ladder on spot instead of futures |  
  
**T39 is the check we never ran, and it may explain everything.** The 0.25 exit  
is a quarter of the distance from the average entry back to the mother candle.  
On a 5-minute mother candle of 30-40 index points at NIFTY 24,000, that target  
is roughly **0.05% of price** — twenty times too small to clear his own cost  
line. His gold and Bitcoin examples are of instruments where a pattern spans  
percents, not hundredths of a percent. **The mother candle may need to be far  
bigger than anything we have tested, or the timeframe far higher, before the  
0.25 exit is even legal under T39.**  
  
**T40 is now a shipped change of direction:** stop costing this on futures.  
  
## Day29 part 1 (LIVE) — the 1% rule, worked in paise  
  
A live session, and it turns Day28's cost lesson into the single most useful  
number in the course. **Everything the backtests have been guessing about the  
0.25 exit is settled here, in his own arithmetic.**  
  
### The arithmetic, as he does it on screen  
  
He states the cost first: **brokerage plus taxes is about 0.2%** — *"0.2  
percentage வந்து brokerage இந்த மாதிரி tax"*. Then the crucial reminder that the  
exit takes only a **quarter** of the distance: *"if you have one percent, how much  
profit do we get? We keep only a quarter of it as profit."*  
  
So with a buy at the 25 and a sell at the 50:  
  
| Distance to target | Quarter of it | Less 0.2% costs | What is left |  
|---|---|---|---|  
| **1%** | 25 paise in a rupee | −20 paise | **5 paise** |  
| **1.5%** | ~37 paise | −20 paise | ~17 paise |  
| **2%** | 50 paise | −20 paise | **30 paise** |  
  
His own words on the first row: *"the distance is one rupee, twenty paise goes to  
brokerage, so we get twenty-five paise in that trade"* — and then flatly, **you  
cannot trade for five paise.**  
  
> **The rule: *"try to keep it so that it is above one percent."*** And on the  
> evidence of his own table, 2% is where it starts being worth doing.  
  
### And the entry needs a real correction, not a drawn one  
  
The live example: the overall market fell **1.12%**, but the trade is taken at  
**0.84%** — and he checks that explicitly before entering. *"If it has not  
corrected, do not take that trade"*, because the brokerage will eat it. So the  
correction is a **precondition**, measured in percent, not a level you drew.  
  
### Three more rules that fall out of the live walkthrough  
  
**The target does not always average.** *"Sometimes the target will not average  
properly"* — because the leg above may be large and the one below small, or the  
reverse. When it does not average at the 50, **look at the next line above the  
50** and trade to that instead. This is the first time he admits the 0.25/0.5  
pair can fail to produce a usable target, and gives the fallback.  
  
**No repeat trade at a level already traded.** *"Do not take repeated trades at  
this same place again"* — his reason is mechanical: each time the market buys up  
through a level and then breaks back down through it, so the second attempt at  
the same price is not the same trade. **Wait for the NEXT boundary.**  
  
**If the new low sits above the previous low, do not start.** The reference has  
to have been broken, not approached.  
  
**And the drawing discipline, repeated:** draw the boundary first, then the  
fibonacci on the V pattern; only **0, 1, 2** on the boundary fib — *"25, 50, 75  
must not be placed"* (Day27p1's rule, said again); and the chart must end up  
looking **clean**, which he treats as a test of whether you have drawn it right.  
  
The Bitcoin worked example needs **three** candles to count the pattern  
(*"three must come"*), not two — worth noting against Day21's two-V rule, which  
this may be restating with the head counted.  
  
### What Day29 part 1 adds  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T41** | The distance from entry to target must be **above 1%**, because the exit takes a QUARTER of it and costs are ~0.2%. 1% leaves 5 paise in a rupee; 2% leaves 30 | **Yes, and it is a hard filter** — reject any campaign whose 0.25 target is under 1% of price |  
| **T42** | Enter only if the market has **actually corrected by ~1%** from the reference — a drawn level with no real correction behind it is not a trade | Yes |  
| **T43** | **No repeat trade at a level already traded.** Wait for the next boundary | Yes |  
| **T44** | When the target does not average sensibly, take the **next line above the 50**, not the 0.25/0.5 pair | Partly — "does not average sensibly" needs a number |  
  
**T41 IS THE ANSWER TO WHY OUR LADDER EARNS SO LITTLE.** Measured on 26-Sep-2026:  
the median 0.25 target in the NIFTY run was **0.221% of price** — and his floor is  
1%. We have been taking, by his arithmetic, the trade he tells the class not to  
take. The mother candles we mark are four to five times too small.  
  
And this reframes the min-unit sweep run the same day. Raising the mother's  
minimum range cut the sample to almost nothing (20 campaigns at 80 points, 6 at  
150, 2 at 400) — which now looks less like a dead end and more like the evidence  
that **NIFTY's 5-minute structure is simply too small for this method.** His own  
instruments are gold and Bitcoin, where a pattern spans percents. T41 says the  
instrument has to move in percents or the method cannot pay for itself.  
  
## Day29 part 2 (LIVE) — why there is no stop loss, and it is not a maths claim  
  
The risk lesson, and it contains **the most important concession in the course**  
alongside the plainest statement of why his system is built the way it is.  
  
### The real reason for no stop loss: CONFIDENCE ARITHMETIC  
  
> *"Do you know what the big plus is in a trade with no stop loss? You keep  
> getting psychologically stronger, because your confidence keeps increasing. If  
> you cut four trades and take the fifth as a winner — your overall confidence is  
> four times DOWN and only one time UP."*

about the trader's psychology**, and he states it as one. A ladder that never  
books a loss never damages the operator; a stop-loss system that wins 20% of the

**And he concedes the other side, explicitly:** *"even trading with a stop loss

measure operator confidence. So the central rule of the system is, by his own  
framing, **outside the reach of any backtest we run** — which explains why every  
control we ran on the ladder came back indistinguishable. We have been testing  
the P\&L of a rule whose stated purpose is psychological.

The strongest language in the whole course, and he says it is from experience:  
  
> *"The price you are going to pay for debt — take it as your life."*

## Day31 (parts 1–3) — PROFESSIONAL FUND MANAGEMENT: the chapter the ladder actually is

|---|---|---|

| 2 | **where your stop loss would have been** | **30%** |  
| 3 | at **twice that distance** | **50%** |

20/30/50 split (T3) which we had mapped onto arbitrary price levels.  
  
### THE RESERVE RULE, finally as arithmetic  
  
The thing Part 4 only gestured at:  
  
> *"If you should have put 1,000 at the first boundary and only placed 500, the

and the third holds 3,000. *"Because it is at under value we put a lot of money  
there — and that is what is safe."*  
  
**Money not deployed at a shallow level is not lost; it accumulates downward.**  
The deeper price goes, the more money is waiting for it. Our model simply capped  
each timeframe's fund and let the unspent part sit idle.

correction is not deep enough to trade. **The instrument's own minimum size  
enforces T42 for free** — no percentage test needed. He shows it failing at 40 and

depth, not on the book's average entry — which makes the second exit a  
structurally different target, not simply a further one.

pre-loaded, which sits oddly beside "15 minutes a day, the rest is advance

## Day33 part 1 — THE CAPITAL FORMULA, at last  
  
The single most important quantitative statement in the entire course, and every  
sizing rule up to now has been circling it.  
  
### capital ÷ 100 = the money for one percent of distance  
  
> *"Capital is the capital you bring yourself. If you have one lakh, then divided  
> by 100... what the capital-divided-by-100 formula is for: how much your capital  
> spends for ONE PERCENTAGE."*  
  
| Capital | Money per 1% of distance |  
|---|---|  
| ₹10,000 | ₹100 |  
| ₹50,000 | ₹500 |  
| ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,000 |  
  
> **SIZE = (capital ÷ 100) × the distance in percent.**  
  
*"That's all — with that the calculation is finished. After that, how much the  
REAL DISTANCE is in the market, that is what we..."*  
  
**And it retro-explains Day31.** That file's worked example was *"0.39 — so 390  
rupees"*, which never made sense against a ₹1,000 account. On ₹1 lakh:  
1,00,000/100 = 1,000 per percent, × 0.39 = **390.** Exact. The formula was being  
used two files before it was stated.  
  
### The percentage budget is SHARED across timeframes and must not double-count  
  
This is the reserve rule's true form, and it is a subtraction:  
  
> *"Already inside this distance we gave 1% on the 5-minute. Now the overall  
> distance on the 1-hour is 5%. So we already took 1 — we take the other 4."*  
  
And the constraint that makes it binding:  
  
> *"Within a distance that is 5%, how can you put 7% of money?"*  
  
**The total money is bounded by the total DISTANCE, and each timeframe gets the  
percentage the smaller clocks have not already consumed inside it.** Our model  
gave each timeframe an independent fixed share (10/20/30/40) and let them overlap  
freely. That is not a simplification of his rule — it is a different rule.  
  
### The 20/30/50, tied to percentages  
  
> *"In these two percent — 20 percent... then 30 percent... and where is the  
> remaining 50? The total distance is two... it goes to 4 percent, and only then  
> does the remaining 50 percent get invested."*  
  
So on a 2% structure the first two legs deploy inside it, and **the 50% leg only  
invests when the distance doubles to 4%** — which is T51's "twice the distance"  
restated in percent rather than in price. Two chapters, same geometry.  
  
**Also:** avoid an inside bar; draw the boundary fib with −1 and 200.

## Day39 — margin, debt, and what the retailer's balance sheet actually looks like  
  
**The professional and the retailer hold opposite balance sheets.** *"The rich man

they all take a loss."* The asset is the ballast; the contract is the bet. Reverse  
the ratio and you reverse the outcome.  
  
**His actual cost of money, stated as rates:** pledging your own holdings costs  
about **6%**; borrowing in a hurry costs **11%**; and if the broker simply hands  
your own money back to you he still takes about **5%**. On a 1 lakh position the  
margin desk asks **20–30%** up front.  
  
**The one condition under which he will borrow:** *"I take a loan at 7% interest,

Borrow only when the spread is roughly 3x the interest — never to fund a contract.  
  
**Why the impatient buyer is destroyed:** *"the market corrects 20%, he buys at  
20,000, and when it sells it sells at 4,000, at 3,000 — he will not have the  
patience for it."* The correction is not the risk. The absence of patience is.

## Day43 — the distance is a RATIO, and the reference point changes the answer  
  
**The whole session is one arithmetic lesson: measure the sub-leg as a FRACTION of  
the total fall, never as an absolute percent.** He measures the total drop, gets  
**0.78**, measures the near leg, gets **0.38**, and divides one into the other. On a  
bigger frame: the near leg is **12%**, the whole is **38%**, and he works the ratio  
out loud — *"38 by 12, how much? 26."*  
  
**And the warning that makes this necessary:** *"this high price is different, this  
price is different — when you take the percentage from THIS price the percentage  
grows; 30 percent of 80, then 30 percent of 60 — the percentage changes depending  
where you take it from."* So the same geometric level yields different percentages  
depending on the anchor. **Fix the anchor or the number is meaningless.**  
  
**Multiple angles, not one entry:** *"think from multiple angles — do not think the  
trade must be taken only here. Take it there, and a minor trade here as well."*  
Whether price goes up or down *"it takes time either way"*, so more than one entry  
point is how the time is used.  
  
| Rule | Testable as stated? |  
|---|---|  
| **T85** | **The distance is a RATIO — the sub-leg divided by the whole fall — not an absolute percentage** | Yes; this is what T34's fib floor already does, now with his arithmetic |  
| **T86** | The same level gives a different percentage from a different anchor, so the anchor must be fixed before measuring | Yes, and it is a real source of error |  
| **T87** | Multiple angles: a major entry AND a minor entry, because time passes either way | Yes; the multi-entry rule of T78 restated |  
  
## Day44 — the closing demo: the entry price is the LOWEST of three candidates

2. **the overall reverse 0.25** — a quarter of the way back

## T95 — INSIDE AND OUTSIDE CANDLE: the close decides

*"If the next candle's CLOSE finished below this candle's body and wick, it is an
